# Electronic Waste: Where AI Hardware Goes to Die

The lifecycle of AI hardware does not end when a GPU becomes obsolete or a server is decommissioned. Electronic waste (e-waste) is the fastest-growing waste stream in the world, and a significant portion of it ends up in informal recycling sites in the Global South, where it is processed by hand under hazardous conditions. This sheet examines the two most documented e-waste sites in the world.

---

## Agbogbloshie — Accra, Ghana

### The World's Most Infamous E-Waste Site

Agbogbloshie, a neighborhood in Accra adjacent to the Old Fadama slum, became globally known as one of the largest e-waste processing sites in the world. At its peak, an estimated 10,000 workers — many of them children and teenagers from northern Ghana — dismantled discarded electronics to recover copper, aluminum, iron, and small quantities of precious metals.

### How It Works

The process is crude and dangerous. Workers break apart devices by hand using hammers and chisels, then burn plastic casing and wire insulation in open fires to expose the metal underneath. Circuit boards are heated over charcoal fires to melt solder and free components. The work requires no capital investment beyond basic tools, making it accessible to the most economically desperate.

### Health and Environmental Consequences

The burning of e-waste releases a toxic cocktail: lead, mercury, cadmium, brominated flame retardants, dioxins, and furans. Studies by the Blacksmith Institute (now Pure Earth) and the Green Cross Switzerland consistently ranked Agbogbloshie among the world's most polluted places.

Research conducted on-site has documented alarming results:

- **Soil contamination:** Lead concentrations in soil at Agbogbloshie were measured at up to 18,125 ppm — over 45 times the USEPA residential soil screening level of 400 ppm.
- **Air quality:** Airborne concentrations of heavy metals and particulate matter far exceed WHO guidelines.
- **Worker health:** Studies found elevated blood lead levels in workers, with children showing levels associated with neurodevelopmental damage. Workers reported chronic respiratory problems, skin lesions, and musculoskeletal injuries.

### The Source of the Waste

Despite international regulations (the Basel Convention prohibits the export of hazardous waste from developed to developing countries), substantial quantities of e-waste continue to reach Ghana. Electronics are often exported labeled as "second-hand goods" or "donations," but a significant fraction — estimated at 15–30% — arrives non-functional and is immediately sent to informal recycling. The European Union, the United States, and the United Kingdom are the primary source regions.

### Recent Developments

In 2021, Ghanaian authorities and international organizations began a partial demolition and remediation of the Agbogbloshie site. Many workers were displaced to other informal sites, raising concerns that the problem was scattered rather than solved. The new Accra Waste Recovery Park aims to formalize recycling, though progress has been slow.

> **Key Statistics**
> - An estimated 10,000 informal e-waste workers at Agbogbloshie at its peak (Basel Action Network, 2018).
> - Lead levels in soil: up to 18,125 ppm, over 45x the safe residential limit (Caravanos et al., 2011).
> - Workers earn approximately $1.50–$3.50 per day from metal recovery (Daum et al., 2017).
> - Ghana receives an estimated 150,000 tonnes of used electronics annually; 15–30% arrive non-functional (SBC/UNEP, 2019).
> - An estimated 60% of Agbogbloshie workers are under 25; a significant number are minors (Blacksmith Institute, 2013).
> - Blood lead levels in Agbogbloshie workers: mean of 9.3 micrograms/dL, with some exceeding 50 micrograms/dL (Caravanos et al., 2014). The CDC reference level for concern is 3.5 micrograms/dL.
> - E-waste burning releases approximately 100+ toxic compounds including dioxins, furans, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (WHO, 2021).

**Sources:** Caravanos, J. et al., *"Assessing Worker and Environmental Chemical Exposure Risks at an e-Waste Recycling and Disposal Site in Accra, Ghana"*, Journal of Health and Pollution (2011, 2014); Daum, K. et al., *"Assessing the economic and environmental impact of e-waste in Agbogbloshie"*, Resources, Conservation and Recycling (2017); Basel Action Network, *"Holes in the Circular Economy: WEEE Leakage from Europe"* (2018); WHO, *"Children and Digital Dumpsites"* (2021); Blacksmith Institute / Pure Earth, *"World's Worst Polluted Places"* (2013).

## Guiyu — Shantou, Guangdong, China

### Asia's E-Waste Capital

Guiyu, a cluster of villages in Guangdong province, China, was for decades the world's largest e-waste recycling site. From the mid-1990s to the mid-2010s, an estimated 150,000 people worked in Guiyu's informal e-waste industry, processing electronics imported from around the world and increasingly from China's own booming domestic consumption.

### Scale and Methods

At its peak, Guiyu processed an estimated 1.5 million tonnes of e-waste per year. The methods were similar to Agbogbloshie but at a much larger scale and with greater specialization:

- **Acid baths:** Circuit boards were dissolved in open vats of hydrochloric and nitric acid to recover gold, silver, and palladium. Workers handled these acids with minimal or no protective equipment. The spent acid, laden with dissolved heavy metals, was dumped directly into rivers and irrigation canals.
- **Open burning:** Cable insulation and plastic casings were burned in the open to recover copper wire.
- **Mercury recovery:** Fluorescent lamps and certain switches were broken by hand, releasing mercury vapor.
- **Toner extraction:** Printer cartridges were emptied manually, releasing carbon black and heavy metal particles.

### Environmental Devastation

The scale of contamination in Guiyu is staggering and has been extensively studied by researchers at Shantou University and international collaborators:

- **Water:** The Lianjiang River running through Guiyu was found to be heavily contaminated with lead, copper, nickel, and cadmium. Drinking water was unusable; residents relied on trucked-in water for years.
- **Soil:** Heavy metal concentrations in agricultural soil exceeded Chinese national standards by 10–100x in multiple studies.
- **Air:** Particulate matter concentrations, especially PM2.5 laden with heavy metals, were consistently above WHO limits.

### Health Impact

Guiyu became one of the most-studied sites for e-waste health effects. Research findings include:

- Blood lead levels in children from Guiyu were significantly higher than in control populations from neighboring towns (mean 15.3 vs. 9.9 micrograms/dL in one study).
- Elevated rates of respiratory diseases, skin diseases, and certain cancers reported in epidemiological studies.
- Abnormally high rates of miscarriage and low birth weight documented.
- Polybrominated diphenyl ether (PBDE) concentrations in breast milk from Guiyu mothers were among the highest ever recorded globally.

### Regulation and Transformation

Beginning around 2015, Chinese authorities undertook a major enforcement campaign to shut down Guiyu's informal recycling operations. Key actions included:

- Construction of a centralized industrial recycling park with controlled processes and pollution controls.
- Forced relocation of informal workshops into the industrial park (with mixed compliance).
- China's 2018 "National Sword" policy banned imports of most e-waste and other recyclable waste, dramatically reducing the inflow of foreign e-waste.

However, environmental remediation of decades of contamination has barely begun. The soil and water contamination persists and will require extensive, costly cleanup over many years.

> **Key Statistics**
> - An estimated 150,000 workers processed e-waste in Guiyu at its peak (Huo et al., 2007).
> - Guiyu processed an estimated 1.5 million tonnes of e-waste annually (various estimates).
> - Blood lead levels in Guiyu children: mean 15.3 micrograms/dL vs. 9.9 in control group (Huo et al., 2007). Current CDC reference: 3.5 micrograms/dL.
> - Lead in Guiyu river sediment: up to 2,458 mg/kg, roughly 6x the Chinese standard (Wong et al., 2007).
> - PBDE levels in Guiyu breast milk: median 58.6 ng/g lipid weight, among the highest recorded globally (Leung et al., 2010).
> - 82% of Guiyu children had blood lead levels exceeding the WHO action level (Zheng et al., 2008).
> - Drinking water had to be imported from 30+ km away due to contamination.

**Sources:** Huo, X. et al., *"Elevated Blood Lead Levels of Children in Guiyu"*, Environmental Health Perspectives (2007); Wong, C.S.C. et al., *"Trace Metal Contamination of Sediments in an E-Waste Processing Village in China"*, Environmental Pollution (2007); Leung, A.O.W. et al., *"Heavy Metals Concentrations of Surface Dust from E-Waste Recycling and Its Human Health Implications in Southeast China"*, Environmental Science & Technology (2008, 2010); Zheng, L. et al., *"Blood Lead and Cadmium Levels and Relevant Factors among Children from an E-Waste Recycling Town"*, Environmental Research (2008).

## The Global E-Waste Picture

The stories of Agbogbloshie and Guiyu are the most visible manifestations of a global problem. Understanding the scale is essential:

> **Global E-Waste Statistics**
> - Global e-waste generation in 2022: 62 million tonnes (UNITAR Global E-waste Monitor, 2024).
> - Growth rate: approximately 2.6 million tonnes more per year.
> - Only 22.3% of global e-waste was formally collected and recycled in 2022.
> - The remaining 77.7% (approximately 48 million tonnes) was landfilled, incinerated, or informally processed.
> - E-waste contains approximately $62 billion worth of recoverable materials annually (gold, silver, copper, platinum, palladium).
> - AI hardware has a notably short lifecycle: data center GPUs are typically replaced every 3–5 years, and AI accelerators can become obsolete in 2–3 years as new architectures emerge.
> - An estimated 2.3 billion people (mostly in Africa and Asia) live in countries without adequate e-waste management systems.

**Sources:** UNITAR, *Global E-waste Monitor 2024*; Forti, V. et al., *"The Global E-waste Monitor 2020"*, United Nations University; ILO, *"The Global Impact of E-Waste"* (2021).

## Conclusion

Electronic waste is the final stage of AI's material lifecycle, and it disproportionately burdens communities in the Global South. The same populations that mine cobalt and coltan often end up processing the discarded devices those minerals were used to build. The rapid obsolescence cycle of AI hardware — driven by the constant pursuit of faster training times and higher throughput — accelerates this flow. Formal recycling infrastructure remains grossly inadequate worldwide, and "solutions" like site demolitions often merely displace the problem. Addressing AI's e-waste footprint requires not just better recycling, but a fundamental rethinking of hardware lifecycles, right-to-repair, and extended producer responsibility.
